From words to numbers

What an LLM does

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini look like they think. Underneath, they do one simple thing:

Guess the next piece of text. Then add it, and guess again.

Try it: what comes next?

The cat sat on the ___

Pick the most likely next word.

An LLM (large language model) does this billions of times, with a huge vocabulary and a lot of practice.

This course explains how it learns to guess so well.

But it wasn't always one model. Let's see how we got here.

How we got here

  1. 2010s
    One model per job Each language task had its own model, trained only for that task.
    Is this review happy or angry?Translate English → FrenchFind the names in this text
  2. 2017
    The Transformer A research paper called “Attention Is All You Need” introduced a new kind of model. Its big surprise: the more data, computing power and size you gave it, the better it kept getting.
  3. 2022
    Chat assistants Scaled-up Transformers, trained on huge amounts of text, became ChatGPT. For the first time, millions of people talked to a language model.
  4. Today
    Agents The same kind of model now writes code, uses tools and works through long tasks on its own.

Everything after 2017 is built on the Transformer. In Lessons 1a and 1b, we build up to it from zero.

Step one: a model only understands numbers. So how do we give it text?

Cutting text into tokens

A model is math. Math works on numbers, not letters.

So first, we cut the text into small pieces called tokens. Then each token gets a number.

The list of all tokens a model knows is its vocabulary.

Is there something in between, with short sequences and no unknown words?

Word pieces: the best of both

Today's LLMs cut text into word pieces: common words stay whole, rare words are built from smaller pieces.

reading → read ing   robots → robot s

The most common method is BPE (byte pair encoding). It learns the pieces from text, with one rule:

Find the two pieces that appear side by side most often. Glue them into one new piece. Repeat.

Every merge adds one piece to the vocabulary and makes the text shorter. We stop at the vocabulary size we want. Today's LLMs stop at around 100,000 to 200,000 pieces, from many languages.

One more kind of token: some don't come from the text at all.

Special tokens

Some tokens don't stand for text. They're signals to the model.

[BOS] [EOS]

Begin and end of a sequence. [BOS] tells the model “start writing”. When the model writes [EOS], it means “I'm done”.

[UNK]

Unknown: stands in for anything not in the vocabulary. Word pieces make this rare.

[PAD]

Padding: fills short sequences so a whole batch has the same length. Computers are fastest with neat rectangles of numbers.

[USER] [ASSISTANT]

Chat models mark who is speaking. Names differ between models; the idea is the same.
