From words to numbers

How we got here

Today's AI chatbots and coding assistants didn't appear overnight. Here's the short story.

  1. 2010s
    One model per task Each language task needed its own model, built and trained just for that task. Most were RNNs, which we meet later in this lesson.
    Sentiment: is this review positive or negative?Machine translation: English → FrenchNamed entities: find the people and places
  2. 2017
    “Attention Is All You Need” A research paper introduced the Transformer. Its big surprise: give it more data, more computing power and more size, and it keeps getting better, much more than older models did.
  3. 2022
    The conversational era ChatGPT launched on 30 November 2022: a scaled-up Transformer you could simply talk to. Millions of people chatted with a language model for the first time.
  4. Now
    The agentic era The same kind of model now acts: coding agents like Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and Antigravity write and run code, use tools and work through long tasks.

Everything since 2017 is built on the Transformer. Lessons 1a and 1b build up to it from zero.

First, what does a language model actually do?

✏️ Quick basics: what an LLM does

ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini look like they think. Underneath, they do one simple thing:

Guess the next piece of text. Then add it, and guess again.

Try it: what comes next?

A cute teddy bear is ___

Pick the most likely next word.

An LLM (large language model) does this over and over, with a huge vocabulary and a lot of practice.

But a model is math. How do we even give it text?

Tokenization

A model works with numbers, not letters. So first, we cut the text into pieces called tokens.

Cutting text into tokens is called tokenization.

One way to cut our example sentence:

A cute teddy bear is reading.

Acuteteddy bearisreading.
  • teddy bear can be one token: a token doesn't have to be one word.
  • The number of tokens is the sequence length, written n. Here, n = 6.
  • All the tokens the model knows make up its vocabulary, written V.
Abookcuteislaughingreadingsoftteddy bear.…

Later, each token becomes a list of numbers, like [0.37, −1.24, …, 0.82]. That's what the model really reads.

So how do we decide where to cut? There are three main ways.

Way 1: one token per word

Try it: type anything. Each word (and each punctuation mark) becomes a token.

By words

🙂 Good

  • Simple
  • Easy to read: each token is a real word

😢 Not so good

  • Huge vocabulary: bear, bears, bear's… each needs its own token
  • Ignores word roots: “bear” and “bears” look unrelated
  • Out-of-vocabulary: a word never seen in training has no token at all

What if we cut smaller instead?

Way 2: one token per character

Try it: the same text, cut into single characters. Spaces show as _

By characters

🙂 Good

  • Small vocabulary: about 100 characters
  • Copes with odd capitals and typos: “TEDDY” or “tedi” is still just letters, so nothing is out-of-vocabulary

😢 Not so good

  • Much slower: sequences get several times longer, and the work grows with length
  • Hard to interpret: a single letter like “e” means very little

Words are too big, letters too small. Is there something in between?

Way 3: pieces of words

Common words stay whole. Other words are built from smaller, common pieces, called subwords.

Acuteted##dybearisread##ing.

## means “this piece glues onto the one before it”: ted + ##dy = teddy.

🙂 Good

  • Uses word roots: read, reads and reading all share “read”
  • Learned from data: the pieces come from real text

😢 Not so good

  • Extra work: we must learn the pieces first
  • Depends on the training text: pieces fit the text they were learned from

This is what almost every LLM uses today. The most common method is BPE (byte pair encoding), with one rule:

Find the two pieces that appear side by side most often. Glue them into one new piece. Repeat.

Each merge adds one piece and makes the text shorter. We stop at the vocabulary size we want. Today's LLMs stop at hundreds of thousands of pieces, from many languages.

One more kind of token: some don't come from the text at all.

Special tokens

Some tokens aren't text. They're signals to the model.

[UNK] Unknown, or “out of vocabulary”

Say our vocabulary is just teddycutebear[UNK]. Someone misspells teddy:

[BOS] [EOS] Beginning and end of sequence, and [PAD] padding

  • [BOS] tells the model “start writing”. When the model writes [EOS], it means “I'm done”.
  • [PAD] fills sequences up to the same length, so many can be processed together as a neat rectangle of numbers.
  • There are more, like tokens marking the user and the assistant in a chat. Names vary between models, for example <|end_of_text|> instead of [EOS].
A chat model is writing an answer. How do we know when to stop?

Now every token has a number. But does that number tell the model what the token means?

✏️ Quick basics: vectors

A vector is just a list of numbers, like [0.9, 0.4].

With two numbers, you can draw it as an arrow: go 0.9 right and 0.4 up.

To measure how similar two arrows are, we use the dot product: multiply matching numbers, then add.

Try it: turn arrow B and watch the dot product.

  • Same direction → dot product close to 1: very similar.
  • At a right angle → 0: nothing in common.
  • Opposite → −1: opposites.

Now: which vector should each token get?

Token representations

Each token needs a vector. Take three tokens: teddy bear, soft and book.

Idea 1: one-hot

Give each token its own position, with a 1 there and 0 everywhere else.

teddy bear[1, 0, 0]
soft[0, 1, 0]
book[0, 0, 1]

teddy bear · soft = 0   teddy bear · book = 0

Every pair scores 0. The vectors say nothing about meaning: teddy bears are soft, but the model can't tell.

Idea 2: learned embeddings

Let the model learn the numbers, so related tokens get similar vectors.

teddy bear[0.90, 0.40, 0.10]
soft[0.95, 0.32, 0.01]
book[0.05, 0.10, 0.99]

teddy bear · soft ≈ 1   teddy bear · book ≈ 0

Now “close” means “related”.

A learned vector like this is called an embedding.

These numbers are made up to show the idea. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of numbers.

With one-hot vectors, how similar are “bear” and “bears”?

To learn embeddings, we need a small neural network. Let's see what that is.

✏️ Quick basics: a tiny neural network

A neural network turns numbers into new numbers, in layers:

  1. Multiply and add. Each layer multiplies its input by a table of numbers called weights.
  2. Scores → chances. At the end, softmax turns raw scores into chances that add up to 100%.
  3. Measure the mistake. The loss is big when the right answer got a small chance.
  4. Nudge the weights so the loss gets a bit smaller. Repeat thousands of times. That's training.
Try it: move the scores. Softmax turns them into chances. The right answer is “cute”.

Loss = −log(chance of the right answer). 100% gives loss 0; small chances give big losses.

Training means: change the weights until the right answers get high chances. Now let's use this to learn embeddings.

Word2vec

Word2vec (2013) learns embeddings with a trick: train a small network on a proxy task, a practice task we don't actually care about.

The task: guess words from their neighbours, over billions of words of text. To do it well, the network must learn what words mean.

Two versions of the practice task. Pick one, then click a word.

  • CBOW (continuous bag of words): the neighbours guess the middle word.
  • Skip-gram: the middle word guesses its neighbours.

Let's look inside the network, using the simplest task: guess the next word.

Inside word2vec: guessing the next word

Our vocabulary has V = 6 tokens: A, cute, teddy bear, is, reading, “.”. The middle layer has d = 2 numbers.

Step through one guess.
Target: the real next word (size V)
Output: guessed chances (size V)
Hidden: the embedding (size d)
Input: one-hot (size V)

Repeat for every word in billions of sentences. The weights keep moving until the guesses get good.

And then something nice happens to the hidden layer.

Embeddings that make sense

After training, we throw the guessing away and keep the hidden layer: each token's embedding.

Words used in similar places end up close together.

Every word starts at a random spot.

With huge amounts of text, embeddings even capture relationships: teddy bear is to soft as Persian poetry is to art. The step from one to the other points the same way.

Word2vec is simple and powerful. But it has two big blind spots.

What word2vec misses

1 · One meaning per word

Every word gets one embedding, wherever it appears.

“What a cute teddy bear!”
“Oh, how cute.” (said with an eye-roll)

“Sit by the river bank.”
“Put money in the bank.”

Same vector, different meanings. The embedding doesn't look at the sentence around it.

2 · Word order is lost

Looking up each word's embedding gives the same set of vectors for:

“The child is hugging the teddy bear.”
“The teddy bear is hugging the child.”

Same words, opposite meanings. Who does what is lost.

Why can't word2vec tell a river bank from a money bank?

We need models that read the whole sentence, in order.
