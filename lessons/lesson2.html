Large language models

Taking the Transformer apart

The Transformer has two halves. The encoder reads the input and builds meaningful embeddings. The decoder writes the output, one token at a time.

It was made for translation, and it worked very well. So people asked: what if we keep only one half?

Try it: keep both halves, or just one.

Let's start with the encoder on its own.

BERT: the encoder alone

BERT keeps only the encoder. Every token looks at every other token, on both sides. That's why it's called bidirectional.

A special token, [CLS], is put at the start. It has looked at every word, so its embedding sums up the whole sentence.

Try it: click a token to see what it looks at.

[SEP] marks the end of a sentence.

How does BERT learn to understand text? With practice games.

BERT's first game: fill in the blanks

BERT first trains on huge amounts of text, with games that aren't the real goal. They're proxy tasks.

In masked language modeling, some tokens are hidden behind [MASK]. BERT must guess them from the words around them.

Try it: click words to hide them (up to two).

🤔 How does guessing hidden words help?
To guess well, BERT must learn grammar, meaning, and which words go together.
And the answers come free: we hid the words ourselves, so nobody has to label anything.

The second game works with whole sentences.

BERT's second game: does it follow?

In next sentence prediction, BERT gets two sentences, split by [SEP]. From the [CLS] embedding, it answers: does the second sentence come right after the first?

Half the pairs come one after the other from real text; half are random. So BERT learns how sentences connect, not just words.

Try it: play BERT. Does the second sentence follow?

After the games, BERT gets its real job.

BERT's real job: fine-tuning

Step 1, pre-training (the two games), is costly and done once. Step 2, fine-tuning, adapts BERT to one task, with a few hundred labelled examples.

A small layer is added on top. It turns an embedding into the answer, and its weights are learned from the examples.

Try it: pick a task and see which embedding gives the answer.

Good results, with little data. But every task needs its own fine-tuning, and BERT has no obvious way to write text.

Now the other half: the decoder alone.

GPT: the decoder alone

GPT keeps only the decoder. It has one job: predict the next token, again and again.

Any task can be written as text for it to continue. So no fine-tuning is needed (though it can still help).

Try it: pick a task, then let the model write.

The original Transformer could write too. What's different?

Encoder-decoder or decoder-only?

Both write the output one token at a time, each token looking only back. The difference is the input.

In an encoder-decoder, the input has its own encoder, where its tokens look both ways. In a decoder-only model, input and output are one sequence, and every token looks only back.

Try it: switch models. Each row is one token; shaded cells are what it can see.

Decoder-only is simpler: no special treatment for the input. It won. Today's LLMs are decoder-only.

Then people made these models bigger. Much bigger.

Scaling laws: bigger is better

In 2020, a study measured it. More training tokens and more parameters give a lower loss, in a smooth, predictable way.

And bigger models learn more from each token: they reach the same loss with fewer tokens.

Try it: move the goal. How many tokens does each model need to reach it?

Curves drawn with a formula shaped like the study's results.

But compute isn't free. How do we measure it?

✏️ Quick basics: FLOPs and FLOP/s

A FLOP is one sum or product of two decimal numbers. Two names look alike:

  • FLOPs (small s): an amount of work. Training an LLM takes about 1025.
  • FLOP/s (per second): how fast a chip works. One modern GPU: about 1015.
Try it: how long does 1025 FLOPs take?

That's at full speed. Real training is slower. This is why LLMs are trained on thousands of GPUs.

With a fixed amount of compute, should we grow the model or the data?

The sweet spot: about 20 tokens per parameter

Compute ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens. So with a fixed budget, a bigger model gets fewer tokens.

In 2022, a study found the best split:
tokens ≈ 20 × parameters
Most big models then were too big for their data: undertrained.

Try it: same compute, different model sizes. Find the lowest loss.
🤔 So why do many models train past 20 tokens per parameter?
Training is paid once. Running the model is paid for every question it answers.
A smaller model, trained on more tokens, costs less to run. So teams often go well past 20.

So what exactly is a “large language model”?

What is an LLM?

A language model gives a probability to a sequence of tokens. In practice: chances for the next token.

Large: billions of parameters or more, trained on hundreds of billions of tokens or more, on many GPUs.

Try it: remove the encoder from the Transformer.

Examples: most LLMs you've heard of.

Bigger is better. But does every token need all those weights?

Mixture of experts

Picture a room of experts. For a maths question, you'd ask the maths expert, not everyone.

A mixture of experts works like this. A small router gives each expert a share for this input. The output is the weighted average of the experts' outputs.

Try it: pick a question, then switch between dense and sparse.

🤔 Are real experts really about maths or biology?
No. Nobody gives them topics. Each one drifts towards the kinds of tokens it handles best, during training.
The names here just make the idea easy to see.

LLMs use sparse MoE: the model can be huge, but each input only pays for a few experts.

Where do the experts go inside an LLM?

Experts inside the LLM

Attention is where tokens talk to each other. The feed-forward network is where most of the computing happens. So the experts are feed-forward networks: the MoE layer takes the feed-forward network's place.

The router chooses for each token, in every layer.

Try it: each colour is the expert a token went to. Click a token to see its router.

🤔 How does the router know which expert to pick?
Nobody tells it. The router has weights, like every other layer, and they're learned in training: nudged after every guess.

Learning to route has a trap.

The trap: routing collapse

Early in training, one expert gets picked a bit more. It gets more practice, so it gets better, so it gets picked even more. The others sit idle, and the model is in effect smaller. This is routing collapse.

The fix: add a balancing term to the loss. It's smallest when tokens are spread evenly:

Lbalance = α · N · Σi fi · Pi
Try it: train, with the balancing term off, then on.

MoEs have grown a lot since. What do today's look like?

MoEs today

Two newer ideas: many small experts (hundreds instead of 8), so each can be very specialised; and shared experts, which run for every token and hold what all tokens need.

Next: changes inside the layers, starting with word positions.

Positions as waves

Attention links tokens directly, so word order is lost unless we add positions. Learned position vectors stop at the longest text seen in training.

The fix was fixed waves, from fast to slow, like clock hands. The nice part: how similar two positions m and n are depends only on how far apart they are:

PE(m) · PE(n) = Σi cos( ωi (m − n) )
Try it: click any cell to compare two positions.

🤔 Why is it biggest when m = n?
Then m − n = 0, and cos(0) = 1, the biggest value cos can have. Every term in the sum is at its top.

But these waves are added to every embedding, so every layer sees them. Who needs them?

Position inside attention

Only attention cares about positions: how far apart two tokens are. The feed-forward network doesn't care if a word is 5th or 12th.

So newer methods leave the embeddings alone and change attention. One way: add a bias to each score. ALiBi takes off more, the farther apart:

score = q · k√d − s · |m − n|
Try it: raise the slope s. Where does “reading” look?

Today's most popular method doesn't add anything. It turns things.

✏️ Quick basics: turning an arrow

To turn an arrow (x, y) by an angle φ, multiply it by a rotation matrix:

cos φ−sin φsin φcos φ × xy

The arrow keeps its length. Only its direction changes.

Try it: drag the angle.

Now let's turn queries and keys.

RoPE: turn queries and keys

RoPE turns each token's query and key by an angle set by its position: position m turns by m · θ.

The score q · k depends on the angle between them. Both turned, so that gap only depends on n − m: how far apart the tokens are, not where they are.

Try it: move the positions. Then shift both together.
🤔 Real queries have many numbers, not 2. How is that turned?
Split them into pairs, and turn each pair like this arrow. Each pair turns at its own speed θ, from fast to slow, like the clock hands.
A bonus: scores tend to get smaller as tokens get farther apart.

Next: does every token need to look at every earlier token?

Sliding window attention

Looking at every earlier token costs more and more as text grows. With sliding window attention, each token only looks at the last w tokens (itself included).

Isn't that the RNN problem again? No: layers are stacked. A token at layer 2 sees tokens that themselves saw further back. So each layer reaches w − 1 tokens further.

Try it: change the window and the layers. How far back can the last token reach?

Image networks have the same idea (a receptive field). Many models mix window layers with full ones.

Another cost: while writing, the model keeps every key and value.

Keeping keys and values

To write each new token, its query is compared with the keys of all tokens before it, then their values are mixed. Those keys and values never change, so they're saved instead of computed again. This store is the KV cache.

It grows with every token, in every head of every layer. Memory fills up.

Try it: write tokens and watch the cache grow (one layer, 8 heads).

Can we keep fewer keys and values?

Sharing keys and values between heads

Each head has its own query, key and value. A query is used once; keys and values are kept. So let heads share keys and values:

  • MHA: every head has its own.
  • GQA: each group of heads shares one. Common today.
  • MQA: all heads share one.
Try it: switch and compare the memory per token.

Last change: where the normalization goes.

Normalize before, not after

Each layer has a residual connection: x skips around the layer and is added back, so learning reaches deep layers.

The original put layer norm after the addition (post-norm), so x itself gets changed. Today it goes before the layer (pre-norm), and x skips by untouched.

Try it: switch, and follow x's skip path (red).

The normalization itself got simpler too.

RMSNorm: a simpler normalization

Layer norm shifts a token's numbers so they average 0, then scales them. RMSNorm skips the shift. It only divides by the root mean square:

RMS(x) = √x12 + … + xd2d   RMSNorm(x) = xRMS(x)

Fewer steps, and it works just as well.

Try it: change a token's numbers and compare.
🤔 Don't both also have learned weights?
Yes. Layer norm then multiplies by learned numbers (γ) and adds learned numbers (β). RMSNorm only multiplies (γ). We leave them out here.

The model is built. Now: how does it pick each word?

Greedy and beam search

The LLM gives chances for the next token. Greedy decoding always takes the most likely one. But the best word now can lead to a worse sentence overall.

Beam search keeps the k most likely sentences so far, where a sentence's chance is its tokens' chances multiplied, and grows each of them.

Try it: run each method on two words.

Beam search finds better sentences, but costs more computing.

Both give the same answer every time. Is that a problem?

Same question, same answer?

Greedy and beam search are deterministic: the same input gives the same output, every time. A chatbot that answers everyone word for word the same feels robotic.

Try it: ask the same question a few times, in each mode.

Sometimes deterministic is what we want: a summary that must come out the same, or a model that grades answers.

How do we add a little chance?

Sampling: top-k and top-p

Instead of always taking the top token, sample: draw one at random, following the chances. A 40% token is picked 40% of the time.

To skip odd picks from the long tail, keep only some tokens, then rescale their chances to add up to 100%:

  • Top-k: keep the k most likely.
  • Top-p: keep the fewest top tokens whose chances add up to at least p.
Try it: “A teddy bear is ___”. Pick a rule, then sample.

Where do these chances come from, and can we tune them?

Temperature

The last layer gives each token a score; softmax turns scores into chances. Temperature T divides the scores first:

pi = exi / TΣj exj / T

Small T: sharper, the top token takes almost everything. Big T: flatter, more surprises. T = 1 is plain softmax.

Try it: drag the temperature.

Why the same input can still differ

With T near 0, every step is fixed maths, so the output should never change. In practice, it sometimes does.

Computers round decimal numbers, so the order of adding matters. Servers group your request with other people's (batching), and that can change the order.

Sometimes we need the output in an exact format.

Guided decoding

Say a program needs the answer as JSON. Asking for JSON in the prompt works most of the time, but not 100%.

Guided decoding only allows tokens that keep the output valid. Every other token gets 0% before picking.

Next: the prompt itself.

The context window

Your prompt goes in. Each token the model writes is added to the input for the next step. Everything it reads at a step is its context; the tokens it writes are output tokens.

So the context limit must hold the prompt and the answer. Today: about 1 million tokens of context, and up to about 100,000 output tokens (for now).

Try it: write the answer and watch the counts.

How much is a million tokens?

How big is a million tokens?

Rough rule: 1 token ≈ 0.75 words, and a page ≈ 500 tokens.

Try it: drag the number of tokens.

More isn't always better. With a very long context, the model has a harder time finding one fact in it: context rot.

The prompt can also teach the model.

In-context learning

You can teach a model inside the prompt, without changing any weights: show a few questions with answers first. This is in-context learning. No examples is zero-shot; a few is few-shot.

Try it: switch between zero-shot and few-shot.

Usually better, but someone must write the examples, and a longer prompt costs more and takes longer.

Example answers, written for this page.

Examples can show how to think, too.

Chain of thought

If the examples show their reasoning, the model writes its reasoning too, before the answer. This chain of thought often gives better answers, and shows how the model got there.

Try it: compare the two prompts.

The cost: more tokens to write, so more money and more waiting. Reasoning models build on this.

One line of reasoning can go wrong. What about several?

Self-consistency

Ask the same question several times, with sampling on, to get several chains of thought. Then keep the answer most of them agree on: a majority vote. This is self-consistency.

Try it: “How old will the bear be next year?”

More reliable, but you pay for every answer.

What matters most

🧱 Decoder-only

An LLM is the Transformer's decoder, made huge: causal attention and feed-forward layers, stacked.

📈 Scale

More parameters and more tokens give lower loss. About 20 tokens per parameter is the sweet spot for training.

🧑‍🏫 Experts

Sparse MoE: a huge model where each token uses only a few experts.

🎲 Picking words

Sampling with top-k, top-p and temperature trades sure answers for variety.
